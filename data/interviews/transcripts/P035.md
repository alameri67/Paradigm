# P035 · Sam Rivera

E-commerce merchandiser · Retail trade

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hello Sam, thanks for making time. I'm a field researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how changes at work affect people's lives. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say in public and what you really think.

**Sam:** Yeah, okay. I do merchandising for an online shop: product pages, what goes where, which items we push. I should warn you, I have opinions about this.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Sam:** I'd say the tools are fine, but who decides how they're used matters. Right now that's the bosses, not us.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Sam:** Honestly, even 'just a tool' has already eaten part of my job. The AI writes the product copy now. I used to write it. So 'no major impact' isn't really how it feels from my desk.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Sam:** I'd keep learning the tools so I'm the one checking and fixing the copy. And I'd keep pushing for a staff group at work so we get a say in how this stuff gets brought in.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Sam:** Uneasy. Even the calm version has already taken something.

**Interviewer:** The card says hiring and pay carry on as normal. Does that change how you see it?

**Sam:** A bit. If pay holds, I can live with it. But I've already watched one part of my job vanish, so I don't fully trust 'normal'.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The tools are fine, but who decides how they're used matters. Right now that's the bosses, not us.”
- **Thinks:** Even the calm version has already taken part of my job. The AI writes the product copy I used to write.
- **Does:** Learns the tools so she's the one checking the copy, and keeps pushing for a staff group with a say in how AI is brought in.
- **Feels:** Uneasy. Something's already been taken.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Sam:** I'd say cutting entry-level jobs is pulling up the ladder. It's a choice companies are making, not the weather.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Sam:** For me, slow means the creative bits keep shrinking until all that's left is checking what the machine made. That's not the job I trained for. And I've still got student debt from training for it.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Sam:** I'd start a course in something the tools are bad at, maybe user research, talking to real customers. I'd also go to the meetings of the retail workers' group in my city.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Sam:** Anxious. It's a slow drip, but it's still a drip.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Cutting entry-level jobs is pulling up the ladder. It's a choice companies make, not the weather.”
- **Thinks:** The creative part keeps shrinking until I'm just checking the machine's work. That's not the job I trained for, and I'm still paying for that training.
- **Does:** Starts a course in user research and joins meetings of the local retail workers' group.
- **Feels:** Anxious, like a slow drip.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Sam:** I'd be loud about it. I'd post, I'd march. These layoffs are about profit, and the people making the money should pay for what it costs everyone else.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Sam:** Privately I think my job's gone in this one. A merchandiser is basically routine office work with a nice title. I'm average at it, not a star, so I wouldn't be the one they keep.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Sam:** I'd cut everything: no takeaway, cancel the subscriptions, ask about pausing my student loan. I'd apply for the new AI jobs even though I hate the idea, and I'd spend evenings organising.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Sam:** Alarmed. My chest gets tight just thinking about it.

**Interviewer:** You'd march against it but also apply for the AI jobs. How do those sit together?

**Sam:** Badly. But I've got rent. You can hate the system and still need to eat in it.

### Summary

- **Says:** “These layoffs are about profit, and the people making the money should pay what it costs everyone else.”
- **Thinks:** My job's gone. A merchandiser is routine office work with a nice title, and I'm average, not a star.
- **Does:** Cuts takeaways and subscriptions, asks about pausing her student loan, applies for AI jobs and organises in the evenings.
- **Feels:** Chest tight. Real fear.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Sam:** I'd say this is exactly what we warned about. Tax the gains, give everyone a basic income, and do it now, not after the next election.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Sam:** Real talk, I'd be out of work within a year and so would everyone in my field. No savings, a flatshare, debt. House prices booming means I'll never own anything. I don't see the way out.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Sam:** I'd sign on for any benefits going, ask my flatmates about splitting costs differently, look for café or warehouse shifts since physical work is safe here, and throw myself into the political fight.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Sam:** Panicked. Like there's no floor.

### Summary

- **Says:** “This is exactly what we warned about. Tax the gains and give everyone a basic income, now.”
- **Thinks:** I'd be out of work within a year, like everyone in my field. No savings, debt, and I'll never own a home. I can't see the way out.
- **Does:** Signs on for benefits, renegotiates costs with flatmates, looks for café or warehouse shifts and throws herself into the political fight.
- **Feels:** Panicked, like there's no floor.
- **Mood:** Panicked

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Sam:** I'd say now it's everyone. Office workers, warehouse workers, care workers. That's actually our chance to stand together.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Sam:** For me, personally, it takes away the café and warehouse shifts I was going to fall back on. So it's worse. But there's a weird bit of hope, because now it's not just people like me fighting for a basic income.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Sam:** Same as before, but I'd spend even more time organising, because the movement would be huge. I'd probably move back in with family if rent got impossible.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Sam:** Still panicked about money. Oddly less alone.

**Interviewer:** You said there's hope in it. Does that change how you'd feel day to day?

**Sam:** Not really. I'd still wake up at 4 a.m. worrying about rent. But I'd have people to be angry with, not just angry alone.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Now it's everyone: office, warehouse and care workers. That's our chance to stand together.”
- **Thinks:** It takes away the café and warehouse shifts I'd fall back on, so it's worse. But now the fight for a basic income isn't just people like me.
- **Does:** Spends even more time organising and would move back in with family if rent became impossible.
- **Feels:** Panicked about money, but less alone.
- **Mood:** Panicked
