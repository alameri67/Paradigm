# P020 · Ingrid Larsen

Site safety manager, commercial projects · Construction

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Ingrid. I'm a researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how people see changes at work, and nothing you tell me will be linked to your name.

**Ingrid:** Of course. I'm happy to help with research, and I'll try to be straight with you.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Ingrid:** I'd say AI cameras have made our sites safer. They spot a missing harness faster than I can. But a person still signs off.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Ingrid:** That version suits me. Better tools, same job. My judgement and my relationships on site are the part no camera does.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Ingrid:** Roll out the camera system on the next two projects. Train my site leads to check what it flags.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Ingrid:** Calm. Good, even.

### Summary

- **Says:** “AI cameras have made our sites safer. But a person still signs off.”
- **Thinks:** Better tools, same job. My judgement and relationships on site are the part no camera does.
- **Does:** Rolls out AI cameras on the next two projects and trains site leads to check what they flag.
- **Feels:** Calm. Good, even.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Ingrid:** I'd say safety rules exist for a reason, and slow change gives us time to write them properly.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Ingrid:** Fine for me. Some of my reporting gets automated, which I'd welcome. I'd worry a little about fewer junior safety officers coming up behind me.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Ingrid:** Keep a trainee on my team even if the budget says no. Help write the company's rules on AI tools on site.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Ingrid:** Calm.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Slow change gives us time to write the safety rules properly.”
- **Thinks:** Fine for me. I'd welcome less reporting, but worry about fewer junior safety officers.
- **Does:** Keeps a trainee on her team and helps write the company's rules on AI tools on site.
- **Feels:** Calm.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Ingrid:** I'd say AI should support decisions on site, not make them. Someone accountable has to sign off.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Ingrid:** My paperwork gets lighter, which is good. Site walks, toolbox talks, which are the short safety briefings before a shift, and the hard conversations with a foreman. None of that is routine office work. I'm not worried for me.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Ingrid:** Use the agents for the reporting and spend more time on site. Push the company to keep a person signing off every permit.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Ingrid:** Calm, mostly. Watching the paperwork side.

### Summary

- **Says:** “AI should support decisions on site, not make them. Someone accountable has to sign off.”
- **Thinks:** My paperwork gets lighter. The site walks and hard conversations aren't routine office work.
- **Does:** Hands reporting to the agents, spends more time on site, and pushes for a person to sign off every permit.
- **Feels:** Calm, mostly.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Ingrid:** Publicly the same. Human sign-off and accountability. And we need a fair way to share the gains.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Ingrid:** Privately, if AI matches professionals, the planning and risk assessment side of my job can be done by a machine. What's left is being on site and being accountable. That's real, but it means fewer of us. I'm good, so I'd probably be one they keep.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Ingrid:** Get my name on the company's AI safety policy. Make sure I'm the person the regulators know. Put a bit more aside.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Ingrid:** Uneasy, but in control.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Human sign-off and accountability, and a fair way to share the gains.”
- **Thinks:** A machine can do my planning and risk work. Fewer of us will be needed, but I'm good, so I'd probably be kept.
- **Does:** Gets her name on the company's AI safety policy, makes sure regulators know her, and saves a bit more.
- **Feels:** Uneasy, but in control.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Ingrid:** I'd say robots on site are a new kind of hazard, and we need clear rules before they arrive, not after.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Ingrid:** This one probably needs people like me more, at least for a while. Robots and people working side by side is hard to get safe. The trades I work with are the ones who'll get hurt, in their jobs and maybe physically.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Ingrid:** Get trained on robot safety standards early. Offer to run the first trial on one of our sites. Talk to the crews honestly about what's coming.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Ingrid:** Busy and needed. Sad for the crews.

**Interviewer:** You sound more settled here than in the last one. Is that right?

**Ingrid:** For me, yes. Robots make my job harder, not smaller. Strange thing to be grateful for.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Robots on site are a new kind of hazard. We need clear rules before they arrive.”
- **Thinks:** This needs people like me more, for a while. The trades I work with are the ones who'll get hurt.
- **Does:** Trains early on robot safety standards, offers to run the first trial on her site, and talks honestly with crews.
- **Feels:** Busy and needed, sad for the crews.
- **Mood:** Uneasy
