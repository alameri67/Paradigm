# P050 · Tasha Brown

Independent financial adviser · Finance and insurance

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hello Tasha, thanks for making time. I'm a field researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how changes at work affect people's lives. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say in public and what you really think.

**Tasha:** Hi there, lovely to talk. I'm an independent financial adviser. Pensions, savings, helping people plan. Most of my clients have been with me for years.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Tasha:** I'd say it's a helpful tool, and I use it to prepare for meetings. But money is personal. People want someone they trust.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Tasha:** The robo-advisers, the apps that invest for you automatically, are already taking my younger clients. That would carry on in this version, slowly. My older clients stay.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Tasha:** Carry on, but put more effort into referrals from the clients I have, like asking them to introduce their grown-up kids.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Tasha:** Mildly uneasy. It's the young ones drifting off.

### Summary

- **Says:** “It's a helpful tool, but money is personal. People want someone they trust.”
- **Thinks:** Robo-advisers, the apps that invest for you, are already taking my younger clients. My older ones stay.
- **Does:** Puts more effort into referrals, asking current clients to introduce their grown-up children.
- **Feels:** Mildly uneasy about the younger clients drifting off.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Tasha:** I'd say a good adviser is more than a portfolio. We're there for the divorce, the redundancy, the death in the family.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Tasha:** Honestly, slow still means my client base gets older and smaller every year. In ten years, who replaces them? People my daughter's age will never think to pay someone like me.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Tasha:** I'd start offering short, cheaper sessions for younger people, maybe a one-off plan instead of an ongoing fee, to get them in the door. And learn to use the AI tools to make those cheap enough to be worth it.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Tasha:** Uneasy. A slow worry about the future.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned divorce and redundancy. Is that from your own life too?

**Tasha:** The divorce, yes. I know what it's like to need a calm voice about money. That's why I think people will still want one.

### Summary

- **Says:** “A good adviser is more than a portfolio. We're there for the divorce, the redundancy, the death in the family.”
- **Thinks:** Slow still means my clients get older and fewer. People my daughter's age won't think to pay someone like me.
- **Does:** Offers short, cheaper one-off sessions for younger people and uses AI tools to make them affordable to run.
- **Feels:** A slow worry about the future.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Tasha:** I'd say redundancy is exactly when people need a human adviser, not an app. I'd be out there saying it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Tasha:** Privately, AI agents that can do routine office work can do a basic financial plan. That's a big chunk of what I charge for. The clients who need me through a crisis will stay, but the routine reviews will look expensive.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Tasha:** I'd specialise in helping people through redundancy: payouts, pensions, what to do next. I'd partner with a local employment lawyer for referrals, cut my office costs and work more from home.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Tasha:** Anxious. But I can see something to aim for.

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Tasha:** I'd say people should get proper advice before they make big decisions in a boom. And the gains should reach ordinary families, not just people who already have shares.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Tasha:** Mixed, really. My older clients would be richer on paper and my fees go up with that. But if AI is as good as a skilled adviser, the next generation won't need me at all. I'm not the best in my field, I'm a good, friendly adviser. That might not be enough.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Tasha:** I'd make the most of the good years. Put more into savings, pay down the mortgage, and keep my daughter's costs covered. I'd also try to stay the human face my older clients call when they're scared.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Tasha:** Anxious. Riding the good part, dreading what comes after.

**Interviewer:** You said you'd specialise in redundancy before. Does that plan still hold here?

**Tasha:** Partly. There'd be loads of redundancies. But the AI could give most of that advice too. It'd be the scared, older clients who still want me.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Get proper advice before big decisions in a boom, and the gains should reach ordinary families.”
- **Thinks:** Older clients get richer and my fees rise, but the next generation won't need me. I'm a good, friendly adviser, not the best, and that might not be enough.
- **Does:** Saves more, pays down the mortgage, covers her daughter's costs and stays the human voice her older clients call when scared.
- **Feels:** Riding the good part, dreading what follows.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Tasha:** I'd say this is when families need to sit down and plan together. Don't panic, don't sell everything.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Tasha:** This one worries me more. With hands-on jobs going too, a lot of my middle-income clients, builders, nurses, people who own small shops, start drawing down their savings just to live. Fewer savings, fewer fees. And if markets crash after the boom, I'm taking calls from frightened people every day.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Tasha:** I'd cut my costs to the bone, keep my emergency fund untouched and talk to my ex about the support for our daughter. I'd keep every client I can by being there when they call.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Tasha:** Alarmed. Holding it together for my clients, worried for us at home.

### Summary

- **Says:** “This is when families need to sit down and plan together. Don't panic, don't sell everything.”
- **Thinks:** Middle-income clients like builders, nurses and shop owners would draw down savings to live. Fewer savings means fewer fees, and a crash means frightened calls every day.
- **Does:** Cuts costs to the bone, protects her emergency fund, talks to her ex about support for their daughter and stays available to every client.
- **Feels:** Holding it together for clients, worried at home.
