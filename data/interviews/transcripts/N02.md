# N02 · Neha Desai

Unemployed 3 months, laid-off UX copywriter (information) · Outside paid work

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making the time, Neha. I'm with the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group. We don't sell anything or campaign for anyone. Nothing you tell me today will be linked to your name. I'll read you five short pictures of how AI might change things over the next five years, and ask how you'd react to each.

**Neha:** Happy to. I'll warn you, I've got opinions. I was a UX copywriter, so I wrote the words in apps, the buttons and the error messages. My whole team got cut three months ago.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it to people in your field, to friends, or online?

**Neha:** I'd say the layoffs were never really about AI. Companies used it as cover to cut staff and please investors. I'd post that, and I'd mean it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Neha:** If AI really stays a tool, I'm fine. I'm good at what I do and I use these tools every day. I'd find a writing job in a few months.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Neha:** Keep up the course I'm doing in UX research, apply for content design roles, and take freelance work to stretch my savings.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Neha:** Annoyed more than scared. It would prove the cuts were greed, not progress.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The layoffs were never really about AI; companies used it as cover to cut staff.”
- **Thinks:** If AI stays a tool, she's good enough to land a writing job within a few months.
- **Does:** Keeps up her UX research course, applies for content design roles and freelances to stretch her savings.
- **Feels:** Annoyed more than scared.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it to people in your field, to friends, or online?

**Neha:** I'd be loud about the junior jobs. If nobody hires beginners, where do the next senior people come from? It's short-term thinking dressed up as innovation.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Neha:** I'm not a beginner, so I'd probably squeeze in. But my kind of writing is exactly the task that disappears. I'd need to be the person who checks the AI's work, not the one who writes.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Neha:** Finish the research course, build a portfolio showing I can test and fix AI-written text, and network hard. I'd give myself six months before taking anything that pays.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Neha:** Uneasy. I can see a path, but it's narrower than the one I trained for.

### Summary

- **Says:** “If nobody hires beginners, where do the next senior people come from?”
- **Thinks:** She'd likely squeeze in, but only by checking AI's writing instead of doing the writing herself.
- **Does:** Finishes her research course, builds a portfolio of testing and fixing AI-written text, and networks hard, with a six-month limit before taking any paid work.
- **Feels:** Uneasy, the path is narrower than she trained for.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it to people in your field, to friends, or online?

**Neha:** I'd say this is a choice, not the weather. A few tech companies decided this. They should pay for it, through taxes, and workers should have a say before tools like this get rolled out.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Neha:** Privately, I know what these tools can do because I use them. Routine writing is gone in this version. The new AI jobs exist, but there'd be a hundred of us for each one.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Neha:** Apply for those AI jobs anyway, testing and safety work. Move somewhere cheaper with a flatmate. And join a union or a campaign group, because I'd be furious.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Neha:** Angry, mostly. Under the anger, pretty scared about money.

**Interviewer:** You're angry at the tech companies but you'd apply to work for them. How does that sit with you?

**Neha:** Badly. But rent is rent. I'd rather be inside making noise than outside with no money.

### Summary

- **Says:** “This is a choice, not the weather; tech companies should pay for it through taxes and workers should have a say.”
- **Thinks:** She knows from daily use that routine writing is gone, and there'd be a hundred people for each new AI job.
- **Does:** Applies for AI testing and safety jobs despite her anger, moves somewhere cheaper with a flatmate and joins a union or campaign group.
- **Feels:** Angry on top, scared about money underneath.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it to people in your field, to friends, or online?

**Neha:** I'd be out marching. Shares boom while people lose their jobs? That's the gains going to the people who already own everything. I'd be pushing for a basic income paid for by taxing those profits.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Neha:** My career's over in this one. Not just mine, my friends' too. I'd have to start again in something completely different.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Neha:** Look seriously at nursing or teaching, something with people in the room. Spend my savings on the training. Organise with other laid-off writers.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Neha:** Like the ground's gone from under me. But at least I'd be angry with a lot of other people.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Shares boom while people lose their jobs; the gains should be taxed to pay for a basic income.”
- **Thinks:** Her writing career, and her friends' careers, would be over, and she'd have to start again in something very different.
- **Does:** Looks seriously at nursing or teaching, spends her savings on training and organises with other laid-off writers.
- **Feels:** Like the ground's gone, but not alone.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it to people in your field, to friends, or online?

**Neha:** Then nobody can say 'just learn a trade'. It's everyone now. I'd say this is the moment to change who owns these machines.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Neha:** Weirdly, I'd feel less alone. Last time it was people like me. Now it's builders and cooks too, so maybe politics actually has to move.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Neha:** I'd still go for work with people, maybe teaching. And I'd put a lot of time into campaigning, because it'd feel like the only thing that could fix it.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Neha:** Scared, but with a strange sort of hope that people finally push back together.

**Interviewer:** Last time you said your career was over. Does that change here?

**Neha:** No, it's still over. It's just that now I wouldn't be the only one telling people it's real.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Nobody can say ‘just learn a trade’ now; this is the moment to change who owns the machines.”
- **Thinks:** With hands-on workers hit too, she feels less alone and thinks politics might finally have to move.
- **Does:** Still aims for teaching or other work with people, and puts a lot of time into campaigning.
- **Feels:** Scared, with a strange hope people push back together.
- **Mood:** Alarmed
