# P080 · Mei Wong

Family doctor · Health care and social assistance

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hi Mei, thanks for making time. I'm from the Work Futures Panel. We're an independent research group, and we don't work for any company, union or party. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say out loud and what you really think.

**Mei:** Of course. I've got the baby monitor on, so if I vanish for a second, that's why.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Mei:** I'd say the AI scribe is the best thing that's happened to general practice in years. It gives me two hours a day back.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Mei:** That world suits me. I get to look at my patients instead of the keyboard and still get home for bath time.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Mei:** Keep using the scribe, keep paying down the med school loans. Maybe help the older doctors in our practice get set up with it.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Mei:** Pretty good, actually. Content.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The AI scribe is the best thing to happen to general practice in years. It gives me two hours a day back.”
- **Thinks:** That world suits her: more time looking at patients and home for bath time.
- **Does:** Keeps using the scribe, pays down her loans, and helps older colleagues set it up.
- **Feels:** Content.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Mei:** I'd say we need to be careful who's accountable when a tool gets a diagnosis wrong. And it shouldn't widen the gap between patients who can pay and those who can't.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Mei:** For me, a slow rollout is fine. Medicine changes slowly anyway. The thing I'd watch is patients coming in sure they've got something rare because a chatbot told them.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Mei:** I'd learn the new tools properly as they arrive, maybe join the practice's working group on them. I'd spend more of each visit untangling what patients read online.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Mei:** Mostly fine. A little tired at the thought of the chatbot visits.

### Summary

- **Says:** “We need to know who's accountable when a tool gets a diagnosis wrong, and it shouldn't widen the gap between patients.”
- **Thinks:** A slow rollout is fine; her worry is patients arriving convinced by chatbot diagnoses.
- **Does:** Learns new tools as they arrive, joins the practice's working group, and spends more visit time untangling online advice.
- **Feels:** Mostly fine, a little weary.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Mei:** I'd say if offices are laying people off, we'll see it in the clinic. Stress, drinking, people skipping their meds because they lost their cover.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Mei:** My job feels safe here. The billing staff and receptionists at our practice are the ones in the firing line, and I like those people. I'd hate watching that.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Mei:** I'd argue at the practice meeting to retrain our front desk staff rather than let them go. And I'd keep an eye out for the AI triage tools the insurers will try to push on us.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Mei:** Uneasy for the people around me, not for me.

### Summary

- **Says:** “If offices are laying people off, we'll see it in the clinic: stress, drinking, skipped meds.”
- **Thinks:** Her own job feels safe, but she'd hate seeing the practice's billing and front desk staff cut.
- **Does:** Argues at practice meetings to retrain front desk staff and watches insurer-pushed AI triage tools.
- **Feels:** Uneasy for the people around her.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Mei:** I'd say medicine is more than getting the diagnosis right. It's knowing the person, their family, what they'll actually do. I'd also say the boom should pay for public health care.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Mei:** Privately, an AI that matches a skilled professional matches me on a lot of what I do. Insurers would love a cheaper chatbot doctor for routine visits. I still owe a lot on my loans, and that's what keeps me up.

**Interviewer:** You said medicine is more than the diagnosis. Do you think patients will see it that way?

**Mei:** Some will. The ones with money and time will. The ones working two jobs will take the free chatbot at midnight. I can't blame them.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Mei:** I'd lean into the parts a machine can't do well, home visits, complex older patients, mental health. I'd pay down the loan faster instead of saving for a bigger house. I'd talk to my partner about what if my income dropped by a third.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Mei:** Anxious. Not panicked, but the ground isn't as solid as I thought.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Medicine is more than the right diagnosis; it's knowing the person. The boom should pay for public health care.”
- **Thinks:** AI could match her on much of her work and insurers would push cheap chatbot visits. Her loans keep her up at night.
- **Does:** Moves toward complex, home and mental health care, pays her loan down faster, and plans with her partner for a smaller income.
- **Feels:** Anxious, the ground less solid than she thought.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Mei:** I'd say we need to think hard about care homes. A robot that lifts someone is fine, but old people need human company or they decline fast.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Mei:** For my own job the robots don't add much. I'm worried about my patients. Lots of them do the hands-on jobs, and those were the ones I thought would be okay. And I'd worry about my baby's future, which is a new kind of worry.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Mei:** Same as before on the loans and the work. I'd probably get more involved in local health politics, pushing for human staffing rules in care homes.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Mei:** Heavy. About the same for me, worse for everyone I see.

### Summary

- **Says:** “A robot that lifts someone is fine, but old people need human company or they decline fast.”
- **Thinks:** Robots add little to her own risk, but her patients in hands-on jobs lose their safe place, and she worries about her baby's future.
- **Does:** Keeps the same loan and work plan and pushes locally for human staffing rules in care homes.
- **Feels:** Heavy, much the same for her, worse for her patients.
- **Mood:** Anxious
