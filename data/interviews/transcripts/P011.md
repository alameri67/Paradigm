# P011 · Helen Brooks

Grid control room operator · Utilities

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Helen. I'm a researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how people see changes at work, and nothing you tell me will be linked to your name.

**Helen:** That's fine. I'm not much of a talker, but I'll do my best.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Helen:** I'd say it's useful for some things. We use it for summaries and weather notes. I wouldn't say much more than that.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Helen:** That's about right for me. A tool that helps, with a person still making the calls on the grid. That's how it should be.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Helen:** Nothing different. Do my shifts, keep up with the drills, help the newest operator settle in.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Helen:** Calm. That version sits fine with me.

- **Says:** “It's useful for some things, like summaries and weather notes.”
- **Thinks:** That's how it should be. A tool that helps, with a person making the calls on the grid.
- **Does:** Works her shifts, keeps up with drills and helps the newest operator settle in.

- **Mood:** Calm

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Helen:** I'd say slow is the responsible way, especially for something like the power grid.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Helen:** Some tasks going is fine, if it's the dull ones. What bothers me is fewer beginners. Control room skills take years. If we don't train people now, who takes over from me?

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Helen:** Speak up in team meetings about keeping the trainee places. Learn the new tools properly so I can tell when they're wrong.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Helen:** Mostly fine. A small worry about who comes after us.

**Interviewer:** You said you'd learn them so you can tell when they're wrong. Do you expect them to be wrong?

**Helen:** Sometimes. Software's fine on a normal day. The grid doesn't fail on a normal day.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Slow is the responsible way, especially for the power grid.”
- **Thinks:** Control room skills take years. If we hire fewer beginners, who takes over from me?
- **Does:** Speaks up for keeping trainee places, and learns the new tools well enough to spot when they're wrong.
- **Feels:** Mostly fine, a small worry about who comes next.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Helen:** I'd say essential services need public oversight. You don't let a company's software run the grid just to save money.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Helen:** Billing and the office side would get hit first. But once a company sees an agent doing office work, it looks at the control room next. Dispatch decisions are where I'd draw the line.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Helen:** Back the union's push for rules on automation in essential services. Ask management, in writing, what the plan is for dispatch.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Helen:** Uneasy. The line I care about is getting closer.

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Helen:** I'd say the gains should go back to the public, and essential services shouldn't be handed to whoever builds the best AI. I'd sign petitions on it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Helen:** If AI matches skilled people at thinking work, it can probably run dispatch on most days. I don't think it's safe on the bad days, but I'm not sure the people deciding would listen to me. And we have two teenagers and a smaller cushion than I'd like.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Helen:** Write down every time the software gets something wrong, so there's a record. Cut back at home and build up savings. Look at whether my skills fit a job with the regulator or in safety.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Helen:** Anxious. Not panicking, but it'd be on my mind every shift.

**Interviewer:** You said it isn't safe on the bad days. Is that the sense of duty talking, or worry about your job?

**Helen:** Both, I suppose. I'd like to think it's mostly duty. I'd be lying if I said the job didn't come into it.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The gains should go back to the public. Essential services shouldn't go to whoever builds the best AI.”
- **Thinks:** It could run dispatch on most days, but not safely on the bad ones. And I'm not sure anyone would listen to me.
- **Does:** Keeps a written record of software mistakes, builds up savings and looks at jobs with the regulator or in safety.
- **Feels:** Anxious. On her mind every shift.
- **Mood:** Anxious

**Helen:** Same as before. Essential services need people, and public oversight.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Helen:** Robots don't add much for me. My work is at a screen. The line crews might change, which makes a storm night harder to picture. But my worry is the same as in the last one.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Helen:** Same plan. Keep the record, keep saving, keep an eye on regulator jobs.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Helen:** About the same. Anxious, but steady with it.
