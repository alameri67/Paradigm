# P056 · Rebecca Stone

Corporate law partner · Professional, scientific and technical services

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Rebecca. I'm with the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group with no ties to AI companies, employers or government. Everything you tell me stays confidential and won't be linked to your name.

**Rebecca:** Fine. I've got forty minutes before a client call, so let's be efficient.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Rebecca:** I'd say we've adopted the tools responsibly and clients get faster work at the same quality. That's the firm line, and it's true.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Rebecca:** In this world, the billable hour bends but doesn't break. We charge a bit differently, maybe fixed fees for routine deals. I'd be fine.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Rebecca:** Push the firm to move more work to fixed fees before clients force us to. Keep my client relationships tight.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Rebecca:** Calm. Mostly a pricing problem.

### Summary

- **Says:** “We've adopted the tools responsibly, and clients get faster work at the same quality.”
- **Thinks:** The billable hour bends but doesn't break. It's mostly a pricing problem.
- **Does:** Pushes the firm towards fixed fees before clients demand it and keeps her client relationships tight.
- **Feels:** Calm. Mostly a pricing problem.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Rebecca:** I'd say the profession has to think hard about how it trains the next generation. That's a real issue, and I'd say it on panels.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Rebecca:** We already halved the junior intake. In ten years we'll have too few senior associates, because we didn't train them. That's a firm problem. My own seat is safe.

**Interviewer:** You said your seat's safe. Is that the same in private as in public?

**Rebecca:** Yes, in this one. Clients hire me, not the firm's software. Ask me again for the fast version.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Rebecca:** Argue at the partners' meeting to keep a small, well-trained junior group, even if it costs us now. Mentor two of them myself.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Rebecca:** Mildly annoyed at my partners' short-term thinking.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The profession has to think hard about how it trains the next generation.”
- **Thinks:** Halving junior intake leaves a gap in senior associates later. That's a firm problem, but her own seat is safe.
- **Does:** Argues for a small, well-trained junior group and mentors two juniors herself.
- **Feels:** Mildly annoyed at her partners' short-term thinking.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. **Rebecca:** I'd say AI frees lawyers to focus on judgement and strategy. And I'd say the layoffs are hitting women and younger staff hardest, which should worry all of us.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Rebecca:** Our paralegals and junior associates are mostly routine work. That's a third of the building. Revenue per partner might even go up for a while, which is grim but true.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Rebecca:** Help plan the cuts, fairly and with decent payouts, because if I don't someone worse will. Put more time into the advisory work clients pay a premium for.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Rebecca:** Uncomfortable. I don't like what I'll be signing.

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Rebecca:** Publicly, I'd say trusted advisers matter more than ever when things are this uncertain. Clients want a person in the room.

**Interviewer:** Your public line is about trusted advisers. Your private one is that the firm doesn't survive. Is that gap hard to hold?

**Rebecca:** No. It's my job to hold it. You don't tell clients the building's on fire while you're still deciding which exit to use.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Rebecca:** Privately, if AI matches a skilled lawyer, the billable hour is dead and the firm shrinks to a handful of partners who hold the clients. I'd probably be one of them. Probably. The firm as I know it doesn't survive.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Rebecca:** Lock in my top clients personally. Talk to my husband about what we'd do if the partnership broke up, though money isn't the issue. Back candidates who want the gains taxed properly, because I think they should be.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Rebecca:** Tense. More about losing what I built than money.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Trusted advisers matter more than ever when things are this uncertain.”
- **Thinks:** The billable hour dies and the firm shrinks to a few partners. She'd probably be one, but the firm she built doesn't survive.
- **Does:** Locks in her top clients, talks through a break-up of the partnership with her husband and backs candidates who want gains taxed.
- **Feels:** Tense, about losing what she built more than money.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Rebecca:** I'd say this is now a question for the whole society, not one profession. I'd be arguing for a proper safety net, funded by those who are gaining.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Rebecca:** For me, not much changes from the last one. What changes is my kids. Both still at school, and I can't name a career I'd tell them is safe. That's new.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Rebecca:** Put money aside in trusts for the children. Stop pushing them towards law. Ask them what they actually enjoy.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Rebecca:** For myself, steady. For my kids, worried.

### Summary

- **Says:** “This is a question for the whole society now. Fund a proper safety net from those who are gaining.”
- **Thinks:** Little changes for her, but she can't name a career she'd tell her children is safe.
- **Does:** Sets up trusts for the children, stops steering them to law and asks what they enjoy.
- **Feels:** Steady for herself, worried for her kids.
- **Mood:** Anxious
