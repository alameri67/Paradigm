## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hi Isabelle, thanks for making time. I'm from the Work Futures Panel. We're an independent research group, and we don't work for any company, union or party. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say out loud and what you really think.

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Isabelle:** I'd say for musicians it's already not a handy tool. The studio work I used to get for ads and games is mostly generated now.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isabelle:** That part of my career has already gone, so this picture just keeps things as they are. Teaching pays the rent. I can live with that, though I miss playing on records.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Isabelle:** Keep teaching, try to add two or three more students, and play the odd wedding or chamber concert. Nothing new.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isabelle:** Sad about what's gone. Settled about the rest.

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Isabelle:** I'd say we need rules, like labels on AI music and pay for the musicians whose playing trained it. That's only fair.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isabelle:** Slow is still bad for me. AI music teachers would get better every year. Parents might decide a tablet app is good enough for the first few years, and those beginners are most of my students.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Isabelle:** I'd work hard on what an app can't do, like ensembles, exam prep and performance nerves. Maybe start a small children's string group. Join the musicians' union campaign on training data.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isabelle:** Anxious, in a slow, grinding way.

### Summary

- **Says:** “We need labels on AI music and pay for the musicians whose playing trained it.”
- **Thinks:** Steadily better AI teachers could take the beginners who make up most of her students.
- **Does:** Focuses on ensembles, exam prep and performance nerves, starts a children's string group, and joins the union's campaign.
- **Feels:** Anxious in a slow, grinding way.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Isabelle:** I'd say this is what happened to artists first, and nobody listened. It needs a public answer, not every person for themselves.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isabelle:** Many of my students' parents work in offices. If they lose their jobs, cello lessons are the first thing to go. I can't blame them.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Isabelle:** Offer group lessons that cost less per child, so families can keep going. Look for teaching posts in schools, which are steadier. Cut our spending at home.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned school posts. Are there many of those near you?

**Isabelle:** Not many. A few part-time ones, and lots of people want them. I'd try, but I'm not counting on it.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isabelle:** Worried. Teaching was the safe part, and it's wobbling.

### Summary

- **Says:** “This happened to artists first and nobody listened. It needs a public answer.”
- **Thinks:** Office-worker parents losing jobs would drop lessons first.
- **Does:** Offers cheaper group lessons, applies for school teaching posts, and cuts spending at home.
- **Feels:** Worried that the safe part is wobbling.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Isabelle:** I'd say if machines do the work, the wealth has to be shared. A basic income would let people like me keep making music that no one has to make money from.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isabelle:** A basic income would honestly save my life's work. But that's the hopeful version. In the meantime, rent goes up with house prices and my students' parents are out of work. I'd be in real trouble.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Isabelle:** Teach anyone who can still pay, online and in person. Busk if I have to. Talk with my partner about moving somewhere cheaper, even though work there is even thinner.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isabelle:** Frightened. Grieving a bit, too.

### Summary

- **Says:** “If machines do the work, the wealth has to be shared. A basic income would let people keep making music.”
- **Thinks:** A basic income could save her life's work, but until then rising rent and out-of-work parents would put her in real trouble.
- **Does:** Teaches anyone who can pay, busks if needed, and discusses moving somewhere cheaper with her partner.
- **Feels:** Frightened and grieving.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Isabelle:** I'd say the same. It's even clearer now that we need to share the gains. I'd play at the protests, honestly.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isabelle:** Robots don't change my job much. I was already down to teaching. It does mean everyone's in trouble, which might make the politics move. Or might make everyone too scared to care about music.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Isabelle:** Same as before. I'd look for any care or community music work, like playing in hospitals or for older people, if there's funding.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isabelle:** Still frightened. Not more than before, just tired.

### Summary

- **Says:** “It's even clearer now that we need to share the gains. I'd play at the protests.”
- **Thinks:** Robots don't change much since she's down to teaching; it might push the politics, or make people too scared to care about music.
- **Does:** Keeps the same plan and seeks funded community music work in hospitals or with older people.
- **Feels:** Still frightened, mostly tired.
