**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Alex:** I'd say even a modest tool is owned by a handful of giant firms, and that's a power problem whether it changes jobs or not.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Alex:** For me personally, not much. I use it to draft grant reports, which frees up time. I'd still be worried about who controls the data and the platforms.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Alex:** Keep running our worker rights programme. Push for transparency rules on workplace monitoring. Use the time saved on grant reports to do more outreach.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Alex:** Uneasy in my usual way. That's my baseline.

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Alex:** I'd say this is a transfer of power from workers to shareholders, plain and simple. Nobody voted for it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Alex:** This is the fight I've trained for. But I also have no savings, and my funding comes from foundations that panic in downturns. And honestly, a lot of what I do is writing and reporting, which these agents do well.

**Interviewer:** You said your job is writing and reporting. Would your organisation replace you with an agent?

**Alex:** Not me, probably, I do the people side too. But the junior staff, the comms person, I could see our board asking. And I'd be the one who has to say no, and maybe lose that fight.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Alex:** Run hard on a campaign for notice periods and severance when firms automate. Diversify our funding so we're not hostage to one donor. Quietly update my CV.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Alex:** Anxious and wired. Scared and fired up at once.

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Alex:** I'd say this is the biggest grab of wealth in history and we have to take it back. Public stakes in AI firms, a wealth tax, a dividend for everyone.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Alex:** Strange truth: this is the moment movements are built for. People would finally listen. But the foundations that pay me are funded by the same stock market that's booming. I'd be arguing against my own funders.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Alex:** Organise every laid-off office worker I can reach. Push for a people's dividend. Look for funding from unions and small donors instead of big foundations.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Alex:** Alarmed, and more alive than I've felt in years.

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Alex:** I'd say once they've automated the care homes and the building sites, the question is who owns the machines. If it's a few billionaires, that's not a market, it's a kingdom.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Alex:** Now the working-class base I organise is hit too, not just office people. That's a much bigger movement, but also a much more desperate one. Desperate people don't always turn left. That frightens me.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Alex:** Build alliances with care workers', builders' and farm workers' groups straight away. Push for public ownership of robots in care. Make sure our people aren't pulled toward blaming migrants.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Alex:** Alarmed. Hopeful about the movement, scared of where anger goes.
