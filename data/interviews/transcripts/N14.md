# N14 · Peter Novak

Full-time carer for a disabled parent, ex-construction · Outside paid work

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for letting me come round, Peter. I'm from the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group. We're not connected to the benefits office, the council or any company. Nothing you say will have your name on it. I'll describe five ways AI might change things over the next five years and ask how you'd react.

**Peter:** That's fine. I'll have to stop if Mum needs me. I look after her full-time, I used to be on building sites. I don't use any AI. Don't really see what it's got to do with me.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it to people you know, to friends, or online?

**Peter:** I'd say good, leave it as it is. Things are hard enough without more change.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Peter:** Doesn't change much. We get by on the carer's money and Mum's benefits. Every month's tight.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, for the household and your mum's care? Be as specific as you can.

**Peter:** Same as now. Look after Mum, watch every bill, keep the forms up to date.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Peter:** Tired, same as always. Worried about money, same as always.

- **Thinks:** It changes little, since they get by on carer's money and his mother's benefits, and every month is tight.
- **Does:** Keeps caring for his mother, watches every bill and keeps the benefit forms up to date.

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it to people you know, to friends, or online?

**Peter:** I'd say it's not fair on young people. Building sites used to take lads on with no qualifications. That's how I started.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Peter:** For me, nothing much. I can't go back to work while Mum needs me. The question is what's there for me after, and I don't know.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, for the household and your mum's care? Be as specific as you can.

**Peter:** Nothing different. Maybe ask about a course I could do from home, but I probably wouldn't get round to it.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Peter:** Same worries as now, really.

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it to people you know, to friends, or online?

**Peter:** I'd say if machines are doing the work, then everyone should get a share. I've read a bit about basic income, money for everyone, no strings. That'd make sense.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Peter:** If the politicians are arguing about it, maybe something like basic income actually happens. That could help people like me and Mum. Or they could cut benefits to pay for the jobless. I don't trust them either way.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, for the household and your mum's care? Be as specific as you can.

**Peter:** Keep an eye on the news. Maybe sign a petition for basic income. Keep caring for Mum and keep the forms in order.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Peter:** Uneasy. A bit of hope, a bit of dread.

**Interviewer:** You said basic income could help, but you don't trust the politicians. Which do you think is more likely?

**Peter:** Honestly, cuts. That's what usually happens to people like us. But I'd still sign the petition.

### Summary

- **Says:** “If machines do the work, everyone should get a share, through something like a basic income.”
- **Thinks:** The argument might bring a basic income that helps him and his mother, but he expects benefit cuts more.
- **Does:** Follows the news, signs a petition for basic income and keeps up his mother's care and forms.
- **Feels:** Uneasy, a bit of hope, a bit of dread.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it to people you know, to friends, or online?

**Peter:** I'd say now's the time. The people making all that money should pay so everyone gets a basic income. Carers too, we work all day.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Peter:** This is where it could go either way for us. Either the money gets shared and we're better off, or prices go up and our benefits don't. I'd guess the second.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, for the household and your mum's care? Be as specific as you can.

**Peter:** Cut back even more. Ask the carers' group about any extra help going. Go to a meeting or two about basic income if there's one in town.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Peter:** Anxious. Every price rise hits us straight away.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Now's the time for the people making money to pay for a basic income for everyone, carers included.”
- **Thinks:** It could go either way, but he expects prices to rise faster than their benefits.
- **Does:** Cuts back further, asks the carers' group about extra help and goes to a local meeting about basic income if there is one.
- **Feels:** Anxious, every price rise hits straight away.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it to people you know, to friends, or online?

**Peter:** I'd say they'd better not use robots as an excuse to cut carers. You can't replace family with a machine.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Peter:** This one scares me. If there's a robot that can lift Mum, maybe they say I'm not needed, and they stop the carer's money. And when Mum's gone, I can't go back to building sites either. There'd be nothing left for me.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, for the household and your mum's care? Be as specific as you can.

**Peter:** Get advice from the carers' group about our rights. Keep a record of everything I do for Mum. Push harder for basic income, because that's the only thing that'd work for someone like me.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Peter:** Frightened. Can't stop thinking about it.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Robots shouldn't be an excuse to cut carers, because you can't replace family with a machine.”
- **Thinks:** A lifting robot could be used to stop his carer's money, and with building work gone he'd have nothing left after his mother.
- **Does:** Gets advice on his rights from the carers' group, records all the care he gives and pushes harder for basic income.
- **Feels:** Frightened, can't stop thinking about it.
- **Mood:** Alarmed
