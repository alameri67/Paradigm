# N09 · Harold Jenkins

Retiree, ex-utility engineer, pension plus investments · Outside paid work

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making the time, Harold. I'm with the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group. We don't sell anything or campaign for anyone. Nothing you tell me today will be linked to your name. I'll read you five short pictures of how AI might change things over the next five years, and ask how you'd react to each.

**Harold:** Glad to help. I spent forty years as an engineer at the power company, so I'm used to people promising the next big thing. I'll admit my investments have done very nicely out of this AI business.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at the golf club or with family, to friends, or online?

**Harold:** I'd say I've seen plenty of these booms. Most new technology ends up as a useful tool, nothing more. Good engineering, sensible prices.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Harold:** My AI shares would probably come off the boil. I'd lose some paper gains, but I'm well covered. Nothing to lose sleep over.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with your money, your family and day-to-day life? Be as specific as you can.

**Harold:** Have a word with my financial adviser about taking some profit and spreading things out a bit. Otherwise carry on. Golf, the garden, the grandchildren.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Harold:** Perfectly fine.

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at the golf club or with family, to friends, or online?

**Harold:** I'd say that's how real change happens. Slowly. Companies have to earn their returns.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Harold:** Steady for my investments, which suits me. The bit about fewer beginners I don't like. My eldest grandson is starting out soon.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with your money, your family and day-to-day life? Be as specific as you can.

**Harold:** Keep the portfolio as it is. Tell my grandson to get a proper apprenticeship or a qualification that needs hands as well as a head.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Harold:** Comfortable. A small worry about the young ones.

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at the golf club or with family, to friends, or online?

**Harold:** I'd say the market will sort it out. New jobs will come, as they always have. The last thing we need is politicians taxing everything that moves.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Harold:** My shares would do well. But the grandchildren are the age where they'd be looking for office jobs. I'm not as sure as I sound that new jobs come quickly enough for them.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with your money, your family and day-to-day life? Be as specific as you can.

**Harold:** Set some money aside for the grandchildren, for training or a deposit. Talk to my son and daughter about what the kids are studying.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Harold:** Pleased about the money, uneasy about the family.

**Interviewer:** You said the market will sort it out, but you're not sure the new jobs come fast enough. Which do you lean towards?

**Harold:** The market, in the long run. But my grandchildren live in the short run, don't they? That's the trouble.

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at the golf club or with family, to friends, or online?

**Harold:** Publicly, I'd say it's not the government's money to hand out. People should be helped back into work, apprenticeships, not paid to sit at home.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Harold:** Privately, I'd be richer than I ever expected, and it'd feel a bit unearned. And my grandchildren's careers could be over before they start. That's the part that bothers me.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Harold:** Uneasy. Odd to be doing so well while the family worries.

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at the golf club or with family, to friends, or online?

**Harold:** I'd say we need to slow down and think. That's not something I'd have said before. Even the trades going? That's a step too far.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Harold:** My advice to learn a trade would be useless. I'd still be comfortable. But I'd lie awake thinking about what my grandchildren will do with their lives, not just for money but for a sense of purpose.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with your money, your family and day-to-day life? Be as specific as you can.

**Harold:** Keep helping them with money, perhaps set up something for each of them. Talk to the family properly about it. Maybe write to my representative, which I've never done.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Harold:** Anxious for the grandchildren. Helpless, which I'm not used to.

**Interviewer:** You said earlier the market would sort it out. Do you still think that here?

**Harold:** I'd like to. But markets sort out prices, not what a young man does with his days. That's different.

### Summary

- **Says:** “We need to slow down and think, and losing the trades too is a step too far.”
- **Thinks:** His advice to learn a trade would be useless; he'd be comfortable but worried about his grandchildren's work and purpose.
- **Does:** Keeps helping his grandchildren with money, may set something up for each, talks with the family and writes to his representative for the first time.
- **Feels:** Anxious for the grandchildren, and unusually helpless.
- **Mood:** Anxious
