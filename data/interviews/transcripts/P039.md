# P039 · Emily Watson

Operations director, logistics provider · Transportation and warehousing

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hello Emily, thanks for making time. I'm a field researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how changes at work affect people's lives. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say in public and what you really think.

**Emily:** Of course. I run operations for a logistics company: warehouses, transport, the lot. I also lead our automation programme, so I'm on the other side of a lot of these questions.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Emily:** I'd say AI is table stakes now. If you don't use it in your bids and your planning, you don't win contracts.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Emily:** That's honestly what I believe. In this picture it's a productivity tool and we squeeze a few percent out of it. Nothing that changes my world.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Emily:** Keep rolling it out in route planning and labour forecasting, and make sure every site manager actually uses the dashboards.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Emily:** Calm. This is business as usual for me.

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Emily:** I'd say slow adoption is a gift for the companies that move early. That's us.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Emily:** If it's slow, the advantage goes to whoever builds the skills first. I'm good at this, and I'd be one of the people others hire to do it.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Emily:** I'd push the board for a bigger automation budget and hire two or three strong data people before everyone else wants them.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Emily:** Calm. Quietly pleased, if I'm honest.

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Emily:** Publicly: we'll redeploy where we can and we'll be transparent with our people. That's the line, and we'd try to mean it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Emily:** Privately, a big chunk of our office staff, the planners, schedulers and customer service teams, wouldn't be needed. I'd be the one signing off on it. That's uncomfortable, but it's my job, and if we don't do it a competitor will.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Emily:** I'd run the restructure in the office teams within twelve months, set up a small retraining track for the strongest people and win two new contracts on the cost savings.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Emily:** Fine for me. Not great about the people part.

**Interviewer:** You said you'd try to mean the transparency line. What would get in the way?

**Emily:** Timing. You can't tell people about cuts before the board signs off, and by then it's nearly done. So 'transparent' tends to mean 'quick'.

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Emily:** I'd say leaders have a duty to manage this responsibly, and that we're investing in our people.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Emily:** This is the first one where I look at my own job. If AI matches a skilled professional, it can plan a network as well as my team, maybe as well as me. I still think I'd be one of the last to go, because someone has to answer to clients and the board. But I'm less sure than I'd like.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Emily:** I'd make myself the face of the transformation to the board and to clients. I'd also move some of our savings out of shares into property and cash, because booms end. And I'd talk to the kids about what they study.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Emily:** Uneasy. Not scared, but I'd notice myself checking.

- **Says:** “Leaders have a duty to manage this responsibly, and we're investing in our people.”

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Emily:** I'd say this is the biggest change in logistics in a generation, and we intend to lead it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Emily:** Oddly, this one's better for me than the last. Every warehouse in the country would need to be rebuilt around robots, and somebody has to plan that, run it and deal with the unions and the safety issues. That's exactly what I've been doing for years.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Emily:** I'd go to the board in month one with a full robot rollout plan and ask for the money. I'd hire engineers and safety people. And honestly, I'd probably get calls from bigger firms, and I'd listen.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Emily:** Calm. Energised, even. The people side still bothers me.

**Interviewer:** The last picture made you uneasy, and this one adds more change. Why do you feel better?

**Emily:** Because in the last one my skills were the thing being copied. In this one, my skills are what everybody suddenly needs. Physical rollouts are messy. Messy is where I'm worth something.
