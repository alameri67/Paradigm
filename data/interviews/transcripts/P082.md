# P082 · Chloe Bernard

Theatre and live-event producer · Arts, entertainment and recreation

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hi Chloe, thanks for making time. I'm from the Work Futures Panel. We're an independent research group, and we don't work for any company, union or party. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say out loud and what you really think.

**Chloe:** Lovely, let's do it. I talk for a living, so stop me if I ramble.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Chloe:** I'd say great, it'll help with the grant forms and the marketing copy. The show itself stays human.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Chloe:** Honestly a handy tool is all I want. It saves my tiny team a day a week on admin, and the stage stays the stage.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Chloe:** Use it for funding bids, ticket emails and scheduling. Put the time saved into finding new writers.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Chloe:** Easy. Good, even.

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Chloe:** I'd say the more screens fill up with machine-made stuff, the more people will want to sit in a dark room with real actors breathing.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Chloe:** That's my bet, and slow change gives me time to build on it. My worry is fewer junior jobs in theatre too. The assistants and interns are how this whole industry trains people.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Chloe:** I'd keep hiring assistants even if the software could do some of it. I'd start branding our shows as live, human-made, nothing faked. Make it part of the ticket.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Chloe:** Quietly excited, actually.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The more screens fill with machine-made stuff, the more people will want real actors in a dark room.”
- **Thinks:** Slow change gives her time to build on her bet, but fewer junior roles would cut how theatre trains people.
- **Does:** Keeps hiring assistants and starts branding shows as live and human-made.
- **Feels:** Quietly excited.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Chloe:** I'd say this is exactly when people need live art. To be together in a room, to laugh at the same time.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Chloe:** Truthfully, it could go either way. Office workers are a big part of our audience. If they're laid off, the cheap seats empty. But the ones who keep their jobs will want something real.

**Interviewer:** You sound upbeat. Is there a version of this that worries you?

**Chloe:** Funding. When politicians panic, the arts budget is the first thing they raid. That's the one that would bite.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Chloe:** I'd add pay-what-you-can nights and pitch a show about all this. Theatre's always done best when it's about what's happening right now. I'd also chase arts funding before the politicians spend it all elsewhere.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Chloe:** Mostly fine. A bit watchful on ticket sales.

### Summary

- **Says:** “This is exactly when people need live art, to be together in a room.”
- **Thinks:** Laid-off office workers could empty the cheap seats, though those still employed may want something real.
- **Does:** Adds pay-what-you-can nights, pitches a show about the moment, and chases arts funding early.
- **Feels:** Mostly fine, watchful on ticket sales.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Chloe:** I'd say the people making fortunes from this should fund culture, the way the old industrialists built concert halls. And theatre should be telling this story.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Chloe:** Two things at once. The rich would have more money than ever to spend on nights out, and my premium bet would look clever. But my regular audience, teachers, office people, would be hurting. I don't want to only make shows for the rich.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Chloe:** Chase wealthy donors hard, and use that money to keep cheap tickets going. Maybe tour smaller shows to towns where people have lost work. I'd keep our three months of savings where they are.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Chloe:** Uneasy. Good for business, bad for the room I want.

### Summary

- **Says:** “People making fortunes from this should fund culture, like the old industrialists built concert halls.”
- **Thinks:** The rich would spend more and her bet would pay, but her usual audience would be hurting, and she doesn't want to only serve the rich.
- **Does:** Courts wealthy donors to fund cheap tickets, tours small shows to hard-hit towns, and keeps her savings intact.
- **Feels:** Uneasy: good for business, bad for the audience she wants.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Chloe:** I'd say when machines can do almost everything, being human together is the point. That's what we sell.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Chloe:** For my work the robots change little. You can't automate an actor. Some of the stagehand and set-building work might go, which I'd fight. My real worry is the whole country feeling lost, and art can only do so much.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Chloe:** Same as before. I'd push to keep our crews human and make that part of the brand. I'd probably produce something about it within the year.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Chloe:** A bit sad. Still believe in what I do.

### Summary

- **Says:** “When machines can do almost everything, being human together is the point. That's what we sell.”
- **Thinks:** Robots change little for her; she'd fight losing human stagehands, and worries most about a country feeling lost.
- **Does:** Keeps crews human as part of the brand and produces a show about it within the year.
- **Feels:** A bit sad, but still believes in her work.
- **Mood:** Uneasy
