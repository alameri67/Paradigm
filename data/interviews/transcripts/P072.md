# P072 · James Whitfield

Tenured history professor · Educational services

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, James. I'm with the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group with no ties to AI companies, employers or government. Everything you tell me stays confidential and won't be linked to your name.

**James:** Very glad to. I should warn you, I've never used one of these AI programs, so I'm speaking from what I read and what my students hand in.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**James:** I'd say, as I do in faculty meetings, that a machine which produces fluent text is not the same as a mind that understands the past.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**James:** Even in this mild version, I see essays that are smooth and empty. Students skip the slow work of reading sources, and that slow work is the whole point of history.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**James:** Go back to handwritten exams and long conversations in my office. Make students show me their notes from the archive.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**James:** Irritated, more than afraid.

### Summary

- **Says:** “A machine that produces fluent text is not a mind that understands the past.”
- **Thinks:** Even now he sees smooth, empty essays from students skipping the slow work of reading sources.
- **Does:** Brings back handwritten exams and office conversations, and asks to see archive notes.
- **Feels:** Irritated more than afraid.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**James:** I'd say universities should defend careful thought, slowly and stubbornly, the way we always have.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**James:** For me, nothing. I'm tenured and a year or two from retiring. Fewer young scholars hired, though, would mean fewer people to carry the subject on. That saddens me.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**James:** Write to the dean about protecting junior posts in the humanities. Finish my last book.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**James:** Sad, but I'm not frightened.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Universities should defend careful thought, slowly and stubbornly, as always.”
- **Thinks:** He's tenured and near retirement, but fewer young scholars means fewer people to carry the subject on.
- **Does:** Writes to the dean about protecting junior humanities posts and finishes his last book.
- **Feels:** Sad, not frightened.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**James:** I'd say governments should regulate this firmly and protect the people losing work. I've believed in a strong state my whole life, and this is why.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**James:** Office work being automated will make parents ask why their children should study history at all. My department would shrink. I'd retire before it reached me, but my younger colleagues wouldn't.

**Interviewer:** You've never used these tools yourself. How sure are you about what they can do?

**James:** Not very sure, if I'm honest. I may be underestimating them, or overestimating them. I'm judging by the essays, and that's a narrow window.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**James:** Speak at the faculty senate. Sign the letters. Help two of my younger colleagues apply elsewhere, where the subject is safer, if such a place exists.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**James:** A heaviness. For the field, not myself.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Governments should regulate this firmly and protect people losing work.”
- **Thinks:** Parents will ask why their children should study history, and his department would shrink. He'd retire first, but younger colleagues wouldn't.
- **Does:** Speaks at the faculty senate, signs open letters and helps younger colleagues apply elsewhere.
- **Feels:** A heaviness for the field, not himself.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**James:** I'd say plainly that a society which hands its thinking to machines owned by a few companies is in danger. The gains belong to everyone.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**James:** If machines match skilled professionals at thinking work, many will ask what a university is for. The humanities would be cut first. And people would stop doing the slow, hard thinking themselves. That's the loss I fear most, more than jobs.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**James:** Retire on schedule, but keep teaching evening classes in town for anyone who wants to read and argue about history. Give more to the parties pushing for public ownership of the gains.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**James:** Grieved. Worried for how people will think.

### Summary

- **Says:** “A society that hands its thinking to machines owned by a few companies is in danger. The gains belong to everyone.”
- **Thinks:** Universities would be questioned and the humanities cut first, but his deeper fear is people giving up slow, hard thinking.
- **Does:** Retires on schedule, teaches evening history classes in town and gives more to parties pushing for public ownership of the gains.
- **Feels:** Grieved, worried about how people will think.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**James:** I'd say we are seeing the largest shift in working life since the factories came, and history tells us those shifts end in either reform or upheaval. We should choose reform.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**James:** For me the robots are beside the point. My wife and I have our savings and my pension. But I've read enough history to know what happens when ordinary people lose their place. It isn't gentle.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**James:** The same as before. Write something for the local paper on what past upheavals teach us, because that's the one useful thing I know how to do.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**James:** Calm about myself. Uneasy about the country.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Shifts this large end in reform or upheaval. We should choose reform.”
- **Thinks:** The robots don't touch him, but history tells him what happens when ordinary people lose their place.
- **Does:** Keeps his plans and writes for the local paper on what past upheavals teach.
- **Feels:** Calm about himself, uneasy about the country.
- **Mood:** Anxious
