# P004 · Priya Nair

Precision-ag data technician · Agriculture (logging and farm support)

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Priya. I'm a researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how people see changes at work, and nothing you tell me will be linked to your name.

**Priya:** Happy to. Fair warning, I think about this a lot, so I might talk too much.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Priya:** I'd say it's a great tool and it's made my drone work much faster, which is true. I'd probably post about the models I'm using.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Priya:** Honestly, I'd be relieved. I've been half expecting the floor to drop out. If it stays a tool, I'm the one who's best with the tool, and that's a good place to be.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Priya:** Keep building my workflows. Learn more of the agronomy side so I'm not just the data person. Maybe ask for a raise.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Priya:** Steady. A bit like I wasted energy worrying.

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Priya:** I'd say it's the realistic version. Farms adopt things slowly, so even strong AI takes years to reach the field.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Priya:** Ten years is a long runway for me. Fewer beginners means fewer juniors, but that's their problem more than mine. I'd feel bad saying that out loud.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Priya:** Get ahead of it. Learn to set up the AI tools myself, not just use them, so I'm the one the company calls when they roll something out.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Priya:** Mostly fine. A little guilty about the juniors.

**Interviewer:** You said you'd feel bad saying that out loud. Why?

**Priya:** Because I got my start in exactly the kind of entry job that would vanish. Somebody took a chance on me.

### Summary

- **Says:** “It's the realistic version. Farms adopt slowly, so even strong AI takes years to reach the field.”
- **Thinks:** Ten years is a long runway for me. The people it hurts are the juniors, not me.
- **Does:** Learns to set up AI tools, not just use them, so she's the one called when the company rolls something out.
- **Feels:** Mostly fine, a little guilty about the juniors.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. **Priya:** I'd say we need to talk about the new jobs, not only the lost ones. And people losing work need real support. I'd mean that.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Priya:** My honest fear is that a lot of what I do is routine. Cleaning image data, making reports for growers. An agent could do most of that. I'm either the person who runs the agents or the person they replace.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Priya:** Push my boss to let me build the agent setup for our clients. Take a course on the field side, crop disease and soil, so I can explain things a model can't. Save harder.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Priya:** Uneasy. The coin flip I keep thinking about feels real now.

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Priya:** I'd probably say less in public, which isn't like me. Maybe something about sharing the gains, since the people who own the models are doing great.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Priya:** If it matches skilled professionals in two or three years, that's me. Being good at my job doesn't help much when the machine is good too. And we don't own a house, so the boom does nothing for us.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Priya:** Sit down with my partner about money straight away. Cut back and stretch our savings. Apply for anything that mixes field work with tech, because the physical side still needs people in this version.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Priya:** Scared, honestly. I'd be losing sleep.

**Interviewer:** In the last one you thought you'd be the one running the agents. What changed?

**Priya:** Speed. With five years I can get myself in position. With two, the agents might not need anyone like me to run them.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The people who own the models are doing great. The gains need to be shared.”
- **Thinks:** If it matches skilled professionals in two or three years, that's me. And we don't own a house, so the boom doesn't help.
- **Does:** Plans money with her partner to stretch savings, and applies for roles that mix field work with tech.
- **Feels:** Scared. Losing sleep over it.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Priya:** I'd say we need to plan for this as a society, now, not after it happens. I'd be loud about that.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Priya:** Oddly, this one gives me a bit of hope. Robots on farms need someone who knows crops, data and machines all at once. That's a short list, and I'm on it. But I could be kidding myself.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Priya:** Go straight to the robot companies selling to farms, and to the farms buying them. Offer to be the person who sets them up and checks the data. Move if I have to.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Priya:** Still worried. But less stuck than the last one.

**Interviewer:** Isn't it odd that adding robots makes you feel better?

**Priya:** A bit. In the last one my job just disappears. In this one there's a new mess, and messes need people. For a while.
