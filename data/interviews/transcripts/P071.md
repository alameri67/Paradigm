# P071 · Karen Mitchell

High school maths teacher · Educational services

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Karen. I'm with the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group with no ties to AI companies, employers or government. Everything you tell me stays confidential and won't be linked to your name.

**Karen:** Happy to help. I've got marking waiting, so this is a welcome excuse.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Karen:** I'd say what I say at parents' evening. It's a fine tool, but you can't learn maths by watching something else do it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Karen:** My job's not the worry. The worry is half my class handing in perfect homework they don't understand. Then they sit the test and fall apart.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Karen:** Move more of the work into the classroom where I can see them think. Fewer take-home problems, more working on the board together.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Karen:** Calm about my job. Frustrated about the learning.

### Summary

- **Says:** “It's a fine tool, but you can't learn maths by watching something else do it.”
- **Thinks:** Her job is fine. The worry is students handing in perfect homework they don't understand.
- **Does:** Moves more work into class where she can see students think, with fewer take-home problems.
- **Feels:** Calm about her job, frustrated about the learning.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Karen:** I'd say, here's why maths still matters. If you can't check the machine's answer, you're at its mercy.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Karen:** Fewer beginner jobs worries me for my students. What are they practising for, if the entry-level job isn't there? I'd need a better answer than I have.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Karen:** Talk to the head about a unit on checking AI answers, using real maths. Talk to my own two at home about what they want to do.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Karen:** A quiet worry. Not about me.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Maths still matters. If you can't check the machine's answer, you're at its mercy.”
- **Thinks:** Fewer beginner jobs make her wonder what students are practising for.
- **Does:** Proposes a unit on checking AI answers and talks with her own children about their plans.
- **Feels:** A quiet worry, not about herself.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Karen:** I'd say this is when we need public schools and good teachers most. I'd back the union on that.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Karen:** Teaching isn't routine office work, so I'm alright. But the district office would cut admin, and someone would pitch AI tutors to save money on teachers. I'd want to see the evidence.

**Interviewer:** You said you're alright. Would you feel that way if the district did bring in AI tutors?

**Karen:** For a while, yes. Thirty teenagers on a Friday afternoon need a person in the room. But I wouldn't be smug about it.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Karen:** Get involved in the district pilot so teachers have a say. Keep a closer eye on our savings in case the school budget gets squeezed.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Karen:** A bit tense. Mostly for the kids.

### Summary

- **Says:** “This is when we need public schools and good teachers most.”
- **Thinks:** Teaching isn't routine office work, but cost-cutting could bring AI tutors and she'd want evidence first.
- **Does:** Joins the district pilot so teachers have a say and watches the family savings more closely.
- **Feels:** A bit tense, mostly for the students.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Karen:** I'd say the gains should pay for schools, health and a safety net, and I'd say it to anyone who'd listen.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Karen:** Teaching might be fine, because parents still want a person with their kids. But if AI matches professionals, what am I preparing students for? And my own two are about to enter that world. That's what would keep me up.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Karen:** Change how I teach, more about reasoning and less about procedures. Help my own kids think about work that needs a person. Join the union campaign on how the gains get shared.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Karen:** Worried, mostly as a mum.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The gains should pay for schools, health and a safety net.”
- **Thinks:** Teaching may be fine, but she doesn't know what she's preparing students for, and her own two children face that world next.
- **Does:** Teaches more reasoning and less procedure, helps her children consider people-centred work and joins the union campaign on sharing gains.
- **Feels:** Worried, mostly as a mum.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Karen:** I'd say the same, louder. And that teachers need a voice in how robots come into schools, not just a memo.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Karen:** Robots don't teach algebra to teenagers, not in five years. I'd still have my job. But my kids' backup options, the trades, would close too. That's harder.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Karen:** Same steps. Encourage the kids to stay close to people: teaching, nursing, anything with a human face.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Karen:** Same worry, with fewer answers.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The gains should be shared, and teachers need a voice in how robots come into schools.”
- **Thinks:** She'd still have her job, but the trades her children might fall back on would close too.
- **Does:** Keeps the same steps and encourages her children towards people-centred work.
- **Feels:** Same worry, with fewer answers.
- **Mood:** Anxious
