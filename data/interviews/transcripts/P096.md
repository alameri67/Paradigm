# P096 · Susan Price

Benefits eligibility caseworker · Government

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hi Susan, thanks for making time. I'm from the Work Futures Panel. We're an independent research group, and we don't work for any company, union or party. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say out loud and what you really think.

**Susan:** That's fine. I'm used to being on the other side of the questions, so this makes a nice change.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Susan:** I'd say the pilot is fine as long as a caseworker makes the final call. The rules are there for a reason.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Susan:** I think I'm all right. Civil service rules protect us, and somebody has to deal with the people the computer can't sort out. Which is a lot of people.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Susan:** Do the training they give us on the new system. Keep doing my cases properly. Nothing else.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Susan:** Settled. Nothing to lose sleep over.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The pilot is fine as long as a caseworker makes the final call. The rules are there for a reason.”
- **Thinks:** Civil service rules protect her, and plenty of cases still need a person.
- **Does:** Does the training on the new system and keeps doing her cases properly.
- **Feels:** Settled.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Susan:** I'd say the agency should take it slow and do it right. People's rent depends on these decisions.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Susan:** Slow suits me. They won't replace us, they just won't replace people when they retire. Our office will get older and smaller. I'm 53, I think I can ride it out.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Susan:** Ask the union rep what the agreement says about new technology. Volunteer to check the AI decisions, since that's where the people will be needed.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Susan:** A little uneasy. Mostly for the younger ones who won't get hired.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The agency should take it slow and do it right. People's rent depends on these decisions.”
- **Thinks:** Retirees won't be replaced and the office will shrink, but at 53 she expects to ride it out.
- **Does:** Asks her union rep about the technology agreement and volunteers to review AI decisions.
- **Feels:** A little uneasy, mostly for younger staff.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Susan:** I'd say through the union that any change has to be negotiated. And that cutting caseworkers when unemployment is rising makes no sense.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Susan:** Routine office work, that's most of my day if I'm honest. Checking forms against rules. But at the same time there'd be more people applying for benefits. So they'd need us, unless the AI handles it. I really don't know which way it goes.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Susan:** Go to every union meeting. Get on the team that reviews the AI's decisions. Put a bit more into savings and hold off on redoing the kitchen.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Susan:** Anxious. I trust the agency, but I've seen budget cuts before.

**Interviewer:** You said you trust the agency. Does that trust hold here?

**Susan:** Mostly. The agency, yes. The politicians who set the budget, less so. They're the ones who'd say, the computer can do it, cut the staff.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Any change has to be negotiated, and cutting caseworkers while unemployment rises makes no sense.”
- **Thinks:** Much of her day is routine rule-checking, though more claimants may keep her needed; she doesn't know which way it goes.
- **Does:** Attends every union meeting, joins the AI review team, saves more, and postpones the kitchen remodel.
- **Feels:** Anxious, trusting the agency but wary of cuts.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Susan:** I'd say we have a duty to the people coming through our doors. They've lost everything and they need a human being to talk to.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Susan:** My office would be flooded. Lawyers, accountants, people who never thought they'd sit in our waiting room. And in a crisis, the politicians would push the AI checks to cope. I'd be busy for a year, then maybe gone.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Susan:** Work the overtime. Learn the AI system inside out, so I'm the person who fixes its mistakes. Talk to my husband about what we'd do if I took early retirement on a smaller pension.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Susan:** Alarmed. Swamped at work and uncertain after.

### Summary

- **Says:** “We have a duty to people coming through our doors. They need a human being to talk to.”
- **Thinks:** Her office would be flooded with laid-off professionals, and the crisis would push AI checks to cope, leaving her busy for a year then perhaps gone.
- **Does:** Works overtime, masters the AI system to fix its mistakes, and discusses early retirement on a smaller pension with her husband.
- **Feels:** Alarmed, swamped now and uncertain after.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Susan:** I'd say the same, only louder. Now it's factory and care workers in the queue too. We can't let a computer decide who eats.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Susan:** The robots don't touch my job. It's the same threat as the last one. But the queues would be twice as long, and the people in them would be my neighbours in this town. That's personal.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Susan:** Same as before. I'd push the union to argue that caseworkers are needed more, not less. And I'd help my own kids think about their jobs.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Susan:** Alarmed. And tired just thinking about it.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Now factory and care workers are in the queue too. We can't let a computer decide who eats.”
- **Thinks:** Robots don't add to her own risk, but the queues double and fill with her neighbours.
- **Does:** Sticks to her plan, presses the union to argue for more caseworkers, and helps her adult kids think about their jobs.
- **Feels:** Alarmed and tired.
- **Mood:** Alarmed
