# N15 · Isaac Boateng

On disability benefit, chronic illness, ex-call centre · Outside paid work

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making the time, Isaac. I'm with the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group. We don't sell anything or campaign for anyone. Nothing you tell me today will be linked to your name. I'll read you five short pictures of how AI might change things over the next five years, and ask how you'd react to each.

**Isaac:** Thanks. I'm on disability benefit at the moment because of my illness. I used to work in a call centre. I use AI tools most weeks, and honestly I've been wondering if they could help me work from home again.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it to friends or online, to friends, or online?

**Isaac:** I'd say it's useful, especially for disabled people. On a bad day it helps me read letters and write emails when my head's foggy.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isaac:** Honestly, that's the version I'd like. Better tools, but the normal jobs still there. Maybe I could do a few hours of customer service from home.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with work, money and your health? Be as specific as you can.

**Isaac:** Look for part-time remote work, maybe ten hours a week to start. Check how it affects my benefit first, because I can't risk losing it.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isaac:** Quietly hopeful, but careful.

### Summary

- **Says:** “It's useful, especially for disabled people, helping read letters and write emails on bad days.”
- **Thinks:** Better tools with normal jobs still there could let him do a few hours of customer service from home.
- **Does:** Checks how work would affect his benefit, then looks for about ten hours a week of remote work.
- **Feels:** Quietly hopeful, but careful.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it to friends or online, to friends, or online?

**Isaac:** I'd say the tools should be built with disabled people in mind, not just to cut staff. That's a real chance to bring people back into work.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isaac:** Slow is okay for me. Call centre jobs would shrink, but not overnight. If I'm careful I could find something part-time before they go.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with work, money and your health? Be as specific as you can.

**Isaac:** Do a short online course in something like customer support software. Talk to a benefits adviser about trying work without losing my support.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isaac:** A bit nervous, but there's a way through.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The tools should be built with disabled people in mind, not just to cut staff.”
- **Thinks:** Call centre jobs would shrink slowly, so he could find part-time work before they go.
- **Does:** Takes a short online course in customer support software and asks a benefits adviser how to try work without losing support.
- **Feels:** A bit nervous, but there's a way through.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it to friends or online, to friends, or online?

**Isaac:** I'd say it's companies cutting the cheapest jobs first, and it's always the same people at the bottom who pay. The government should be making them hire disabled people for the new roles.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isaac:** The tools that could help me work are the same ones taking call centre jobs. That's the bit I can't get past. The remote job I was hoping for wouldn't exist.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with work, money and your health? Be as specific as you can.

**Isaac:** Apply for the new AI jobs anyway, like checking what the assistants get wrong with customers. Lean harder on the benefits adviser. Cut what I spend, which isn't much already.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isaac:** Anxious. The door I was looking at just closed a bit.

**Interviewer:** You said AI could help you work again. Does that still feel true here?

**Isaac:** It still helps me. It just doesn't help me get paid. That's different.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Companies cut the cheapest jobs first, and the government should make them hire disabled people for the new roles.”
- **Thinks:** The tools that could help him work are taking call centre jobs, so the remote job he hoped for wouldn't exist.
- **Does:** Applies for new AI jobs like checking assistants' mistakes with customers, leans on his benefits adviser and cuts spending further.
- **Feels:** Anxious, the door he hoped for closed a bit.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it to friends or online, to friends, or online?

**Isaac:** I'd say the gains belong to everyone. A basic income would treat disabled people as people, not as a problem to be checked up on every year.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isaac:** With all the office workers out of work, nobody's hiring someone like me. And the benefit system would be swamped. They'd tighten the rules, and people like me always get squeezed first.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with work, money and your health? Be as specific as you can.

**Isaac:** Keep every medical record ready for when they review me. Join a disabled people's group pushing for basic income. Put the job hunt on hold, really.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isaac:** Scared. My health gets worse when I'm stressed, too.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The gains belong to everyone, and a basic income would treat disabled people as people, not as a problem to check.”
- **Thinks:** With office workers out of work nobody would hire him, and a swamped benefit system would tighten the rules on people like him.
- **Does:** Keeps his medical records ready for review, joins a disabled people's group pushing for basic income and pauses the job hunt.
- **Feels:** Scared, and stress makes his health worse.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it to friends or online, to friends, or online?

**Isaac:** I'd say the same, louder. Now it's everybody, so maybe people will finally listen about a basic income.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Isaac:** For me the robots don't change much. I wasn't going to do a warehouse job anyway. If anything, a robot helper at home could make my bad days easier. But the money worry stays the same.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year, with work, money and your health? Be as specific as you can.

**Isaac:** Same as before. Keep my records ready, keep pushing with the group. Maybe find out about home help robots, if they're cheap.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Isaac:** Still scared. But with a bit of company in it now.

**Interviewer:** You said a robot helper could make things easier. Does that make this picture better or worse for you than the last one?

**Isaac:** About the same. Easier days, same empty bank account.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Now it's everybody, so maybe people will finally listen about a basic income.”
- **Thinks:** Robots change little for him, could even help on bad days, but the money worry stays the same.
- **Does:** Keeps his records ready, keeps campaigning with the group and looks into cheap home help robots.
- **Feels:** Still scared, but with company now.
- **Mood:** Alarmed
