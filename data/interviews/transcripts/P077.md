# P077 · Angela Moore

ICU registered nurse · Health care and social assistance

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hi Angela, thanks for making time. I'm from the Work Futures Panel. We're an independent research group, and we don't work for any company, union or party. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say out loud and what you really think.

**Angela:** Sure. I'm just off a run of night shifts, so forgive me if I'm blunt.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Angela:** I'd say, great, now can it do my charting? Half my shift is typing what I already did.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Angela:** Nothing changes for me, which is kind of the problem. We're short staffed and burned out, and a better search engine doesn't fix that.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Angela:** Same as now. Pick up the extra shifts when they beg, try to see my three kids awake, and keep looking at day jobs I'll never apply for.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Angela:** Tired. Not scared, just tired.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Great, now can it do my charting? Half my shift is typing what I already did.”
- **Thinks:** Nothing changes, and that's the problem. A better search engine doesn't fix short staffing or burnout.
- **Does:** Keeps picking up extra shifts, tries to see her kids, and browses day jobs without applying.
- **Feels:** Tired, not scared.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Angela:** I'd say if AI takes the paperwork off nurses, bring it on. But keep it away from the bedside.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Angela:** Slow is fine by me. Nobody's replacing an ICU nurse in ten years. What scares me is fewer new grads being hired. We already can't keep the young ones, and then who covers my nights?

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Angela:** I'd push our unit to pilot the charting tools and I'd volunteer to test them. I'd also mentor the new grads we do get, so they don't quit in year one.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Angela:** A bit hopeful, honestly. Maybe the job gets lighter.

### Summary

- **Says:** “If AI takes the paperwork off nurses, bring it on. Keep it away from the bedside.”
- **Thinks:** No one replaces an ICU nurse in ten years, but fewer new grads means nobody to cover her nights.
- **Does:** Volunteers to pilot charting tools and mentors the new grads her unit does hire.
- **Feels:** A bit hopeful the job might get lighter.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Angela:** I'd be at the union meeting saying the same thing. Use AI for the forms, not to cut nurse ratios. Safe staffing first.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Angela:** Office layoffs don't touch me directly. But I know hospital managers. If they save money on admin, they'll look at us next and say the AI monitors can watch two more beds each.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Angela:** I'd get more involved with the union and write down every time an alarm or an AI flag gets it wrong, so we have proof. And we'd put a bit more into savings, just in case.

**Interviewer:** You said you'd log the mistakes. Have you ever seen one of these tools get it wrong?

**Angela:** Oh yes. The sepsis alert fires on half the unit and misses the one who's actually crashing. You learn to look at the patient, not the screen.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Angela:** Wary. Like when a new manager starts and smiles too much.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Use AI for the forms, not to cut nurse ratios. Safe staffing first.”
- **Thinks:** Office layoffs don't touch her, but she expects managers to use AI monitors as an excuse to give each nurse more beds.
- **Does:** Gets more involved in the union, logs every wrong AI flag as evidence, and saves a little more.
- **Feels:** Wary, like a new manager who smiles too much.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Angela:** I'd say nurses aren't going anywhere, and the people getting rich off this should pay for proper care. Everyone else losing their job will still get sick.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Angela:** Honestly? My job is probably safer than my friends' jobs. But a lot of people losing work means a lot of people losing health cover and turning up sicker. That lands on my unit.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Angela:** Keep my head down and keep working. Probably more overtime, because the ICU would be fuller. I'd tell my oldest to look hard at nursing or a trade.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Angela:** Safe, but bracing for a rough few years at work.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Nurses aren't going anywhere, and the people getting rich off this should pay for proper care.”
- **Thinks:** Her job is safer than her friends', but more people losing cover means sicker patients landing on her unit.
- **Does:** Keeps working, likely takes more overtime, and steers her oldest toward nursing or a trade.
- **Feels:** Safe, but bracing for a rough few years.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Angela:** I'd say a robot can turn a patient, fine, but it can't notice the look on someone's face before they go downhill. I'd say that loudly.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Angela:** This is the one that gets me. I don't think a robot does my job. But I think a hospital board buys robots to lift and fetch and then decides one nurse can cover six beds. That's how people die.

**Interviewer:** Earlier you said nobody replaces an ICU nurse. Does this change that?

**Angela:** Not replace. Thin out. They won't fire me, they'll just give me more patients until I quit. Same result.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Angela:** I'd fight the ratios with the union, hard, maybe strike if it came to it. And I'd sit down with my husband and look at what we'd do if I walked away from the bedside.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Angela:** Angry and scared for my patients more than for me.

### Summary

- **Says:** “A robot can turn a patient, but it can't see the look on someone's face before they go downhill.”
- **Thinks:** She doesn't think a robot does her job, but expects boards to buy robots and then stretch each nurse across six beds.
- **Does:** Fights nurse ratios through the union, up to striking, and plans with her husband for leaving bedside work.
- **Feels:** Angry and scared, mostly for her patients.
- **Mood:** Anxious
