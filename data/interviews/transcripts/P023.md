## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Arjun. I'm a researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how people see changes at work, and nothing you tell me will be linked to your name.

**Arjun:** Sure, happy to. I install the robots, so I'm probably at the optimistic end of the people you talk to.

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Arjun:** I'd say it's a useful tool and people should learn it. Same as I'd say about any new tool.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Arjun:** This version's fine for me. I'd still be installing robot cells, and the AI just helps me write code and documents faster.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Arjun:** Keep doing installs. Take a course on the newer vision systems. Maybe pitch a side project at work.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Arjun:** Calm. A bit of 'is that all?'

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Arjun:** Same line. Retrain, adapt, and the market will create new jobs. Maybe with some help for people in between.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Arjun:** Here's what nags me. If AI matches skilled engineers at thinking work, a lot of my design and programming is done by the AI. What's left is being on the floor with my hands on the equipment. Still a job, but not the one I trained for.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Arjun:** Spend more time on the floor and less at the desk. Get really good at the hands-on side. Put some money from the share boom into robotics companies.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Arjun:** Uneasy, which surprises me.

### Summary

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Arjun:** I'd say this is the biggest opportunity in a generation for people who build robots. And everyone else can learn to work alongside them.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Arjun:** For me, this is the best case. Every warehouse, farm and kitchen needs robots installed, tuned and fixed. That's me. I'd be in demand for a decade. I know it's rough for the people the robots replace, but they can retrain.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Arjun:** Start my own integration firm, probably. Hire a few technicians from the plant who want to learn. Charge a lot.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Arjun:** Excited. Really excited, honestly.

**Interviewer:** You said they can retrain. Picture a 41-year-old line worker with two kids and no savings. How does that work for her?

**Arjun:** Honestly? I don't know. Paid training, I'd say. But I can't really picture her doing my job. I guess I don't think about that part much.
