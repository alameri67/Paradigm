# P041 · Jordan Kim

Software developer · Information

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Hello Jordan, thanks for making time. I'm a field researcher with the Work Futures Panel, an independent group that studies how changes at work affect people's lives. Nothing you tell me will be linked to your name, so feel free to tell me what you'd say in public and what you really think.

**Jordan:** Sure. I'm a junior software developer. These days I mostly review code that an AI agent writes, which is a weird way to spend your first few years in a career.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Jordan:** I'd say the tools are great and make me faster. That's what everyone in tech says, and it's true.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Jordan:** Even in the calm version I'm a bit rattled. My company froze hiring last year. If AI is 'just a tool' and they still froze hiring, what happens when it's more than that?

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Jordan:** I'd keep shipping, get really good at reviewing AI code and try to own one part of the system that nobody else understands.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Jordan:** Uneasy. Background hum of worry.

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Jordan:** I'd say we're making a mistake if we stop training juniors. In five years there'll be no seniors.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Jordan:** Fewer beginners is literally my situation. I'm in the door, but I'm average, and the gap between me and a senior with an AI agent is huge. I'm not sure I'd get promoted before the ladder gets pulled up.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Jordan:** I'd study the parts of the job AI is worst at: system design, debugging production problems, talking to customers. I'd start putting a few hundred a month aside, which means cutting back on going out.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Jordan:** Anxious. I check the job boards at night, which doesn't help.

**Interviewer:** You'd argue for training juniors. Is that a principle or self-interest?

**Jordan:** Both. It's true for the industry. It's also me. I'd be lying if I said it wasn't about me.

### Summary

- **Says:** “We're making a mistake if we stop training juniors. In five years there'll be no seniors.”
- **Thinks:** I'm average, and a senior with an AI agent does more than I do. I might not get promoted before the ladder's pulled up.
- **Does:** Studies system design and production debugging, and saves a few hundred a month by going out less.
- **Feels:** Anxious, checking job boards at night.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Jordan:** I'd say we need a real safety net now: a basic income, or at least long unemployment support. This isn't about people being lazy.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Jordan:** If AI matches skilled professionals, it definitely matches a junior developer. I'd be out early. No savings to speak of, rent in a big city, and a whole industry of more experienced people looking for the same few jobs.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Jordan:** I'd cut down to essentials right away, look into moving back home, and apply for anything technical with a physical side, like setting up hardware or networks, because that's safe in this picture.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Jordan:** Alarmed. I'd barely sleep.

**Interviewer:** Earlier you said you could see a door. Is it gone here?

**Jordan:** Pretty much. The door was the AI jobs. In this one, the AI does those too.

### Summary

- **Thinks:** If AI matches skilled professionals, it matches a junior dev. I'd be out early with no savings and big-city rent.

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Jordan:** Same as before. A safety net, and the gains shared, because now it's everyone.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Jordan:** The hardware fallback gets squeezed too, I guess. But there's a tiny bit of hope: robots need a lot of software, and the messy kind, real-world stuff. If any coding jobs survive, that's where they'd be.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Jordan:** Same cuts as before. I'd go hard on robotics: learn the tools, apply to every robotics firm, take a pay cut if I had to.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Jordan:** Still alarmed. A little less hopeless, oddly.
