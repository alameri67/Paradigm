# P060 · Aisha Bello

Architect · Professional, scientific and technical services

_Simulated interview. The person is a made-up persona, and the answers are a model's guess at how someone like them would react. They aren't real quotes._

## Opening

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Aisha. I'm with the Work Futures Panel, an independent research group with no ties to AI companies, employers or government. Everything you tell me stays confidential and won't be linked to your name.

**Aisha:** Yes, happy to. I've been arguing about this with my colleagues all week, so good timing.

## S0 · No major impact

**Interviewer:** Picture this. AI stays a handy tool, like a better search engine. Hiring, pay and prices carry on the way they would have anyway. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Aisha:** I'd say the design tools are fun. I can try fifty layouts for a site in an afternoon and pick the best three.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Aisha:** I love it, honestly. It takes away the boring drafting and leaves me the part I trained for, which is deciding what a place should feel like.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Aisha:** Get really good at the tools. Run a lunch session at the office to show the others what I've learned.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Aisha:** Happy. A bit excited, even.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The design tools are fun. I can test fifty layouts in an afternoon and pick the best three.”
- **Thinks:** It takes away the boring drafting and leaves the part she trained for.
- **Does:** Gets really good at the tools and runs a lunch session to teach colleagues.
- **Feels:** Happy, even a bit excited.
- **Mood:** Calm

## S1 · Capable but slow

**Interviewer:** Now picture this. Very capable AI exists, but it spreads slowly, over ten years or more. Some tasks disappear, but few whole jobs do. Companies hire fewer beginners. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Aisha:** I'd say architecture has always changed with its tools. Pencil to computer, computer to AI.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Aisha:** Fewer graduates hired worries me. I learned by doing the dull drawings under someone senior. If the AI does those, how do the young ones learn to see a building?

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Aisha:** Offer to mentor a student. Keep pushing at work for a proper training role for juniors, even if it's small.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Aisha:** A small worry, mostly for others.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Architecture has always changed with its tools. Pencil to computer, computer to AI.”
- **Thinks:** She learned by doing dull drawings under someone senior. If AI does them, juniors won't learn to see a building.
- **Does:** Mentors a student and pushes for a proper junior training role at work.
- **Feels:** A small worry, mostly for others.
- **Mood:** Uneasy

## S2 · Moderately fast

**Interviewer:** Next one. Within 5 years, AI assistants that can act on their own handle most routine office work. There are layoffs and pay pressure in office jobs. Some new AI-related jobs appear, and politicians argue loudly about what to do. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Aisha:** I'd say big developers will use this to cut corners and squeeze fees, and someone has to stand up for good buildings, for the people who live in them.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Aisha:** A lot of my week is routine: drawings, planning paperwork, specs. If the agents do that, the firm needs fewer of us. I'm good, not exceptional. I could be one of the cuts.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Aisha:** Join a design union or the professional body's AI group. Start building a small portfolio of my own projects, like home extensions, in case I need to go out on my own.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Aisha:** Anxious. It's my craft, not just my job.

### Summary

- **Says:** “Big developers will use this to cut corners and squeeze fees. Someone has to stand up for good buildings.”
- **Thinks:** Much of her week is routine and she's good, not exceptional, so she could be cut.
- **Does:** Joins her professional body's AI group and builds a small portfolio of home-extension work on the side.
- **Feels:** Anxious. It's her craft, not just her job.
- **Mood:** Anxious

## S3 · Fast

**Interviewer:** Now a faster version. Within 2 to 3 years, AI matches skilled professionals at most thinking work. Lots of office and professional workers lose their jobs. Share and house prices boom. There's a political crisis over who should get the gains. Physical jobs are mostly untouched. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Aisha:** I'd say the gains from this should pay for homes people can actually afford, not go into more shares. Housing is the crisis, and we know how to build it.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Aisha:** If AI designs as well as a skilled architect, clients will buy a design for a fraction of my fee. Architecture becomes a commodity, like I feared. I'd lose the job and the thing I love doing.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Aisha:** Lean hard into the local, human side: home extensions, community projects, people who want to sit at a table with someone. Cut spending. Talk with my husband about who earns what if I'm out.

**Interviewer:** You said community projects. Do those pay enough to live on?

**Aisha:** No, not usually. That's the honest answer. It might be more about keeping my hands in the work than paying the bills.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Aisha:** Grief, almost. And fear about money with a little one.

### Summary

- **Says:** “The gains should pay for homes people can afford, not more shares. We know how to build them.”
- **Thinks:** Clients will buy AI designs for a fraction of her fee. Architecture becomes a commodity and she loses work she loves.
- **Does:** Leans into local home extensions and community projects, cuts spending and talks money with her husband.
- **Feels:** Almost grief, plus fear about money with a young child.
- **Mood:** Alarmed

## S4 · Fast AI and fast robots

**Interviewer:** Last one. Everything in the previous picture happens, and on top of that, general-purpose robots arrive in warehouses, kitchens, building sites, farms and care homes within 3 to 5 years. Hands-on work is no longer a safe place to be. If this happened, what would you say about it at work, to friends, or online?

**Aisha:** I'd say now it's everyone's problem, from the architect to the bricklayer. We should be on the same side.

**Interviewer:** And just between us, off the record, what do you really think it means for you?

**Aisha:** Strangely, if robots make building cheap, there could be far more building. Someone still has to stand in front of a community and say why this place should look like this. I don't know if that's me. I hope so.

**Interviewer:** What would you actually do in the first year? Be as specific as you can.

**Aisha:** Same plan as before. Maybe reach out to builders I know about working together, design and build, small and local.

**Interviewer:** How does it feel, picturing that?

**Aisha:** Still scared, but a small bit of hope crept in.

### Summary

- **Says:** “It's everyone's problem now, from the architect to the bricklayer. We should be on the same side.”
- **Thinks:** Cheap robot building could mean far more building, and someone still has to explain to a community why a place looks the way it does.
- **Does:** Keeps her plan and reaches out to builders about small, local design-and-build work.
- **Feels:** Still scared, with a small bit of hope.
- **Mood:** Anxious
